## Scope
This document defines shared decision making and describes how clinicians should apply it when more than one reasonable option exists.

## Recommendations
- Shared decision making is a process in which clinicians and patients work together to choose tests, treatments or care plans, based on clinical evidence and on the patient's values and preferences.
- Follow six steps: invite the patient to participate, present the options, discuss benefits and harms, elicit the patient's preferences, reach a decision together, and revisit the decision when circumstances change.
- Use decision aids written in plain language when they are available for the condition.
- Document the options discussed, the patient's stated preferences and the final decision in the record.

## Notes
Shared decision making is most useful when evidence is uncertain or when options carry different trade-offs. It is not required for emergency care where the patient cannot take part.

## Preparing for the conversation
Check which options are reasonable for the patient and what the evidence says about each. Allow at least 20 minutes for a first conversation about a major decision. Offer the patient a written summary and a chance to bring a family member or interpreter.

## Presenting risks
Use natural frequencies such as 5 in 100 rather than percentages. Present benefits and harms in the same format and over the same time period. Use visual aids such as icon arrays. Avoid vague words like rare or common unless they are defined.

## Checking understanding
Ask the patient to say in their own words what the options are. This teach-back approach finds misunderstandings early. If the patient is unsure, offer more time and a follow-up visit instead of pressing for a decision.

## Decisions that change
Offer to revisit the decision at set points such as after test results or at three months. Record when the patient changes their mind and why. A decision to decline treatment is a valid decision and the patient should still receive other care.
