## Scope
This document explains how WHO guideline groups rate the certainty of evidence and the strength of recommendations.

## Recommendations
- Rate the certainty of evidence as high, moderate, low or very low.
- Evidence from randomised trials starts as high certainty. Evidence from observational studies starts as low certainty.
- Downgrade certainty for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.
- Upgrade certainty for a large magnitude of effect, a dose-response gradient, or when plausible confounding would reduce the observed effect.
- Issue a strong recommendation when the benefits clearly outweigh the harms. Issue a conditional recommendation when the balance is uncertain or values and preferences vary.

## Notes
State the certainty rating next to every recommendation so readers can see how firm the evidence is.

## Certainty of evidence
High certainty means further research is very unlikely to change confidence in the estimate. Moderate means it is likely to have an important effect. Low means it is very likely to change the estimate. Very low means any estimate is very uncertain. Rate certainty for each outcome separately.

## Strength of recommendation
A strong recommendation means most people would want the course of action and it can be adopted as policy in most situations. A conditional recommendation means most people would want it but many would not, so a decision needs discussion. Both types state the direction clearly.

## Evidence to decision
Panels consider the balance of effects, resource use, equity, acceptability and feasibility before deciding on direction and strength. The table that summarises these judgements is published with the guideline. Costs are reported in local currency where possible.

## Good practice statements
A good practice statement is used when the benefit is large and obvious and little evidence exists because researchers would never study it. Such statements are rare. They are labelled separately and do not carry a certainty rating.
